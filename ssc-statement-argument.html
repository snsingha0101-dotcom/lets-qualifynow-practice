Statement & Argument

Learn how to identify strong and weak arguments using logic, relevance and practical exam-oriented reasoning.

📘 What is Statement & Argument?

In Statement & Argument questions, a statement or issue is given followed by one or more arguments. You have to determine whether each argument is strong or weak.

Important: A strong argument directly addresses the issue and provides a logical, relevant and practical reason. A weak argument may be irrelevant, emotional, vague or based on an unsupported assumption.

These questions test your ability to evaluate reasoning rather than simply agree or disagree with an opinion.

🔍 What is an Argument?

An argument is a reason given in support of or against a particular statement, proposal or issue.

Strong Argument:
Relevant + Logical + Practical + Directly related to the issue.
Weak Argument:
Irrelevant + Emotional + Personal + Vague + Unsupported.
Key Point:
Do not judge an argument merely by whether you personally agree with it. Judge its logical strength and relevance to the issue.

💪 Characteristics of a Strong Argument

  • It is directly related to the statement or issue.
  • It gives a logical reason.
  • It addresses the actual problem.
  • It is practical and reasonable.
  • It considers relevant consequences.
  • It is supported by the information given or by reasonable logic.
  • It does not depend mainly on emotion or personal preference.
Strong Argument Formula:

Relevant + Logical + Practical = Strong Argument

⚠️ Characteristics of a Weak Argument

  • It is unrelated to the main issue.
  • It is based mainly on emotion.
  • It is vague or unsupported.
  • It attacks a person instead of addressing the issue.
  • It makes an extreme or unnecessary claim.
  • It relies only on personal preference.
  • It does not provide a meaningful reason.
Weak Argument Formula:

Irrelevant + Emotional + Unsupported = Weak Argument

🧠 How to Identify a Strong Argument

1

Understand the Issue

First identify exactly what the statement is asking or discussing.

2

Read the Argument

Understand what reason is being presented in support of or against the issue.

3

Check Relevance

Ask whether the argument directly addresses the issue.

4

Check Logic

Ask whether the reason makes logical sense.

5

Check Practicality

Determine whether the argument has practical significance.

6

Ignore Personal Bias

Do not decide based only on your personal opinion.

✍️ SSC-Level Solved Examples

Example 1

Statement:

Should schools conduct regular mock tests for competitive examination preparation?

Argument:

Yes, mock tests can help students practise solving questions within a limited time.

✓ Strong Argument
It is directly related to the proposal and gives a practical reason for conducting mock tests.
Example 2

Statement:

Should schools conduct regular mock tests for competitive examination preparation?

Argument:

No, students should not take tests because examinations are difficult.

✗ Weak Argument
The argument does not provide a logical reason against mock tests. The difficulty of examinations alone does not address the usefulness of practice tests.
Example 3

Statement:

Should public libraries provide more digital learning resources?

Argument:

Yes, digital resources can provide users with access to a wider range of learning material.

✓ Strong Argument
It directly addresses the benefit of digital learning resources and gives a relevant reason.
Example 4

Statement:

Should a city improve public transport during peak hours?

Argument:

Yes, additional transport services may help manage increased passenger demand during busy periods.

✓ Strong Argument
It is directly connected to the issue and provides a practical reason.
Example 5

Statement:

Should companies provide safety training to new employees?

Argument:

No, because new employees have different names.

✗ Weak Argument
The reason is irrelevant to the issue of safety training.
Example 6

Statement:

Should students revise previous-year questions before an examination?

Argument:

Yes, reviewing previous-year questions can help students understand the types and patterns of questions asked in an examination.

✓ Strong Argument
The argument is relevant, logical and directly connected to examination preparation.

📚 Important Types of Arguments

1. Practical Argument

Focuses on practical benefits, consequences or implementation.

2. Logical Argument

Uses a clear relationship between the issue and the reason.

3. Social Argument

Considers relevant effects on society or a particular group.

4. Economic Argument

Focuses on relevant financial costs, benefits or resource use.

5. Emotional Argument

Relies mainly on feelings rather than logical reasons. Such an argument is generally weak when it does not address the issue logically.

🎯 Three-Point Test for Arguments

1

Relevance

Does the argument directly relate to the issue?

2

Logic

Does the argument provide a sensible reason?

3

Practicality

Does the argument have practical significance?

✓

Final Check

If all three are reasonably satisfied, the argument is generally strong.

Fast Method:

Relevant + Logical + Practical = Strong

If an argument is irrelevant or mainly emotional and unsupported, it is generally weak.

🔄 Argument vs Personal Opinion

Argument:
Provides a reason that supports or opposes the issue.
Personal Opinion:
Simply expresses what someone likes, dislikes or believes without giving a relevant reason.
Example

Issue: Should students practise mock tests?

Opinion:

"I personally do not like mock tests."

Weak
This is a personal preference and does not provide a relevant reason about the usefulness of mock tests.

Argument:

"Mock tests allow students to practise completing questions within a limited time."

Strong
This provides a relevant and practical reason.

❌ Common Mistakes

1. Confusing Agreement with Strength

An argument does not become strong merely because you agree with it.

2. Ignoring Relevance

A true statement can still be a weak argument if it does not address the issue.

3. Choosing Emotional Reasons

Emotional language alone does not make an argument logically strong.

4. Accepting Personal Preference

Personal likes and dislikes are usually weak unless they are directly relevant to the issue.

5. Ignoring Practicality

A strong argument should have meaningful practical relevance to the issue.
